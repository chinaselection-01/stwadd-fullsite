An audit is not a box to tick on a supplier scorecard. It is the difference between a factory that can hit your specification and one that can only show you a catalogue. The mistake most first-time importers make is treating "factory audit" as a single event — when in practice there are two very different checks, and confusing them is how a buyer ends up with a trading office that rents a floor for the day you visit.

01 Two Audits, Not One

Before you schedule anything, separate the two questions an audit answers:

  • Capability audit — can this supplier actually make your product, to your spec, at your volume, batch after batch? This is about machines, process control and QC.
  • Social compliance audit — is the workplace run in a way you can defend to your own customers, your retailer, and your conscience? This is about labour, safety and environment.

A supplier can pass one and fail the other. A shiny, compliant office with no real production line is a capability fail. A skilled workshop with no documented labour standards is a compliance gap. Score them separately.

03 Social Compliance Audit: The People Side

This is what your own retailer or distributor will ask about, and what protects you if a journalist or a competitor ever asks where your goods come from. The recognised frameworks here are third-party audits — SGS, TUV, BV, Intertek, and BSCI on request.

AreaWhat to checkEvidence to ask for
Working hours & wagesLegal limits, paid overtimePayroll vs attendance records
Age verificationNo underage labourID checks on file for every worker
Health & safetyGuarding, PPE, fire exitsSafety training log, inspection record
Environment & permitsWaste, coatings, dischargesValid operating and environmental permits
CertificationsManagement & social systemsISO 9001, and a third-party social audit report (SGS / TUV / BV / Intertek / BSCI on request)
Be precise about certifications. A supplier that says "we are BSCI-certified" should be able to show the current report. If they cannot, what you have is a claim, not a certificate. For your own order you can require a fresh third-party audit and name the standard in the contract.

04 Documents to Request Before You Fly

A site visit is expensive; don't waste it discovering what a PDF could have told you. Request these first and read them before booking a flight:

  • Business licence — confirms a real, registered entity and its scope.
  • ISO 9001 — a baseline quality-management system (management, not a product certificate).
  • Material and food-contact certificates — SGS / LFGB for the steel, coating and any plastic in contact with liquid.
  • Export records — bills of lading or customer references that prove they actually ship the product type you want.
  • Any audit report — capability or social, current and named.

05 Red Flags That Disqualify a Supplier

You do not need a 40-page report to walk away. Any of these, on their own, is enough to keep looking:

Stop and reconsider if you see: no fixed production address; refuses to show a working line; only ever meets you in a trading office; no QC records at all; cannot explain how vacuum insulation is achieved; subcontracts the whole order without telling you; or answers certification questions with "don't worry about it".

06 The Audit-Rights Clause for Your Contract

Copy this and fill the brackets. The point is to make the audit a committed condition, not a favour:

Contract clause

Audit rights: The buyer has the right to conduct or commission a capability and/or social compliance audit of the production premises before and during the production of [order reference]. The supplier shall provide access to the actual production line, QC records, and relevant certifications (including ISO 9001 and, where required, a third-party social audit such as SGS, TUV, BV, Intertek or BSCI). If the audit reveals a critical capability or compliance failure, the buyer may withhold payment, require corrective action, or cancel the order without penalty. The cost of a buyer-commissioned third-party audit is borne by [the party named].

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a capability audit and a social compliance audit?
A capability audit checks whether the supplier can actually make your product to spec, at your volume, consistently — equipment, QC system, capacity and sample-to-bulk consistency. A social compliance audit checks the people side: working hours and wages, age verification, health and safety, environmental permits, and certifications. They answer two different questions: 'can they make it?' and 'should you be sourcing from them?'
Can I require a third-party social audit such as BSCI or SGS?
Yes. For your order you can require a recognised third-party social compliance audit — SGS, TUV, BV, Intertek, or BSCI on request — and ask the supplier to share the report. Put the requirement and the acceptable standard in the contract before production starts, so it is a committed condition, not a polite request.
Do I need to visit the factory in person, or can the supplier's own documents suffice?
For a first order, request the documents first (business licence, ISO 9001, material and food-contact certificates, export records, any audit report) and read them before you travel. A site visit still matters for the capability audit because photos can hide a line that is subcontracted or shared — but documents save you from flying to a trading office that shows you someone else's floor.
How often should a supplier be re-audited?
A capability audit is worth repeating when something changes — a new product line, a move, or a switch to a different production line. A social compliance audit is typically refreshed every one to two years, or sooner if you receive a specific concern. Build the re-audit interval into the framework agreement rather than relying on memory.
