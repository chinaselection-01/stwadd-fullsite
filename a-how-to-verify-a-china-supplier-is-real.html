How to Verify a Chinese Supplier Is Real: A 7-Step Due Diligence Checklist

A business licence photo proves almost nothing. Any company can produce one, and a genuine licence can belong to a factory that never touches your product. Here is a due diligence sequence that actually exposes whether the supplier behind the website is real, written for buyers of drinkware and houseware who are committing container-sized money to a company they have never visited.

Why a Licence Photo Is Not Verification

Suppliers know that buyers want to see a business licence, so they send one. The document is genuine more often than not, and it is still nearly worthless as evidence, because registration answers a different question from the one you are actually asking. Registration tells you that an entity with a legal name exists. It does not tell you that the entity has a workshop, that it owns the equipment, that it employs the people shown in the video, or that it has ever exported a container before.

There is a second failure mode that catches experienced buyers. A real licence belongs to a real company that buys from other factories and sells you the output. Nothing in that document is fraudulent. The company is simply not the producer, and if you have priced your supply chain assuming the producer is your counterparty, you have built your margin forecast on a false premise. You cannot audit your way out of that later, because the quality system belongs to someone you never contracted with.

Step 1 — Look the Company Up in the Official Registry

Search the company name at gsxt.gov.cn, the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System. It is the Chinese state registry and it is free. The record gives you registration status, registered capital, the legal representative, the date of establishment and any administrative penalties or abnormal-operations listings.

Read three specific fields rather than stopping at the fact of registration. First, the business scope: it should cover the manufacture or trading of products resembling yours. A company whose scope lists only electronic components and cultural-creative goods is an unusual source for vacuum flasks. Second, registered capital and establishment date: a company registered eleven months ago with 300,000 RMB of capital is a different proposition from one operating since 2017. Third, any abnormal-operations record, which in China signals that the company failed to submit filings or was subject to a court-ordered change, and which buyers routinely fail to notice.

If the name on the website does not return a clean match, stop. Most fraudulent suppliers operate under a trading name, a brand, or a name that differs from the registered entity by a suffix. Ask for the registered Chinese name and the unified social credit code, then match those against the database yourself rather than accepting a PDF.

Step 2 — Match the Signatory to the Registered Legal Representative

The contract, the invoice, the quotation and the payment account must all trace back to the same legal entity, and that entity's legal representative should be the person who signs. When a quotation comes from one company name and the proforma invoice requests payment to a different company, or to an individual, you have found the single most common structure in supplier fraud.

Pay particular attention to the beneficiary account. A Hong Kong entity, an offshore company, or a personal account appearing as the payee on a contract signed by a mainland factory is a hard stop, not a negotiation point. It can be legitimate in cross-border structures, but it must be disclosed and papered deliberately. If it appears without explanation, assume the money is being routed somewhere you cannot follow and stop the transaction.

Step 3 — Ask for Export Records Instead of Accepting "We Have Exported Before"

Export history is the hardest evidence to fake convincingly, and the easiest to request. Ask for the export licence numbers, the destination countries and approximate dates of the last few shipments. Cross-check the exporter name against the name on your quotation. If a supplier claims to ship regularly to the United States but appears nowhere in the public record under that identity, weigh the claim accordingly.

For drinkware specifically, ask which test reports accompany the goods and who issued them. Reports traceable to a specific model, a specific material grade and a specific production batch tell you the supplier has faced a buyer's laboratory before. A generic certificate that covers no model number is decoration.

Step 4 — Make Them Walk the Phone Through a Working Line

A video tour is easy to fake and easy to test. The giveaway is control. A showroom gives you one continuous, well-lit, uncluttered walk. A working production floor does not: the camera shakes, someone walks in front of the lens, a machine runs loud enough to make people shout, and the tour has to dodge around stacked cartons.

Run the call like a checklist rather than a tour. Ask them to show a machine running and to put a finished unit next to a fresh one from the line. Ask them to walk to the raw material store and show the stainless steel sheet or plastic pellets, then show the purchase invoice for that material. Ask to see the packing area and the warehouse. Ask them to hold the camera still on a wall of production tickets, and to read one aloud so you can hear other operators in the background.

Then take control back. Ask a question whose answer you already know from your earlier research, and ask it at the end of the call. A guide who can answer first-time questions with a specific number, a specific machine brand or a specific date is standing in a factory they know well.

Step 5 — Commission a Third-Party Audit Before the Deposit

A third-party factory audit is the only step that produces evidence you did not generate yourself, and it costs a few hundred US dollars against a container that routinely runs five figures. Buyers routinely treat this line item as optional and then discover, after the goods are in the water, that the production capacity described in the quotation does not exist.

Order the audit yourself rather than accepting the supplier's recommendation of an inspection agency, since a supplier who picks the auditor picks what the auditor sees. For drinkware, the audit scope should include the actual production equipment, evidence of the company's own QC process, whether the inspection and packing stations are in-house, and how the company stores finished goods. Ask for the report in English before you pay, not after.

If the supplier objects to an independent audit, take that seriously as a signal. Reasonable suppliers ask questions about scope and cost. Suppliers who refuse outright are telling you something specific about their willingness to be examined.

Step 6 — Buy a Sample Before You Buy the Container

The sample order is the cheapest available test of whether a supplier intends to deliver what was agreed. It costs a few hundred dollars and a few weeks, and it reveals more than any certificate. You learn whether the specification you were given in writing matches the physical product, whether the company communicates on schedule, and whether it treats a small order as real business or as an obstacle.

Test the sample for the properties you actually sell on, not for the properties the supplier volunteered. For an insulated bottle that means the insulation test under a defined standard rather than an impression; for a product with contact surfaces it means US FDA food-contact testing, with LFGB for European destinations and Proposition 65 review for California. For a new model, weight and capacity measured on a scale and in a graduated cylinder usually disagree with the quoted figures, and a supplier who will not accept that finding is not a supplier you want handling a repeat order.

Once the sample is approved, seal it, label it with a signature and date, and reference it in the purchase order. That sealed sample is what you argue from when the bulk goods arrive different from what you approved.

Step 7 — Structure the Payment So You Can Still Recover

Verification is only half the job; the payment structure determines whether the verification mattered. For a first order, the safest structure is an irrevocable letter of credit opened for the balance against shipping documents. If a letter of credit is impractical at your volume, the fallback is a deposit of around thirty percent with the balance payable against a copy of the bill of lading.

Avoid full prepayment to an account that does not match the contracting entity, and avoid payment methods with no practical recourse. Wire transfers against the correct corporate account provide a paper trail. Western Union, cash deposits and payments to a personal account leave you with nothing to enforce. Whichever route you take, the beneficiary name on the bank advice must match the legal entity on the contract, character for character.

For Chinese New Year, factories slow down sharply, usually for two to four weeks, and payment terms agreed close to the holiday are harder to enforce because the counterparty is closed when you need to talk. Build that window into your schedule rather than discovering it.

Put the Verification Work Into the Contract

Verification that is not written down decays within a few months and disappears entirely when a dispute starts. The purchase order should carry the registered company name and unified social credit code, the full material specification, the approved sealed sample reference, the defect tolerance, the inspection standard, the production lead time stated as a contractual deadline, the payment milestones, and the governing law and dispute forum.

Two clauses are worth the effort of a lawyer and pay for themselves in one dispute. The first is a right to audit: a clause stating that you or your appointed inspector may inspect the production premises and the in-process goods during production, on notice. The second is a substitution clause stating that any material or component change requires your written approval in advance, which prevents a factory swapping steel grade or resin grade mid-production to save cost while staying within the letter of the original specification.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I check whether a Chinese company is genuinely registered? Search the company name in the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System at gsxt.gov.cn. It is the official state registry and it is free. Check registration status, registered capital, establishment date and the legal representative, then read the business scope and any penalties or abnormal-operations listings rather than stopping once you see the record appears.

Is a business licence photo proof of anything? No. Licence images are trivially obtained or forged, and more importantly a genuine licence can belong to a company that buys from other factories and never produces your product. A licence proves that a legal entity exists. It does not prove capacity, equipment, quality control or export history. You need registry records, export records, a live production-line video and ideally an independent audit.

What is the safest payment method for a first order? An irrevocable letter of credit opened for the balance against shipping documents is the strongest protection. If that is impractical, pay roughly thirty percent as a deposit with the balance against a copy of the bill of lading. Whatever you choose, the beneficiary account name must match the contracting entity exactly, and you should avoid full prepayment, personal accounts and transfers with no recourse.

What should I do if a supplier refuses a video tour or a third-party audit? Treat it as a significant negative signal rather than an inconvenience. Reasonable suppliers ask about scope, duration and cost, and many have audits done routinely. Suppliers who refuse outright are indicating that they do not want an independent party confirming what happens behind the showroom. In our experience the correct response is to walk away, not to raise the offer.

How long does full due diligence take before placing a first order? Budget three to six weeks. Registry and export checks are same-day. A live video audit and a sample order usually run one to three weeks depending on tooling and revision rounds, and a third-party factory audit adds several days on top. The Chinese New Year period can add two to four weeks to any timeline that touches it. Attempting to compress this sequence is what creates the risk you were trying to avoid.
