Factory Direct vs Trading Company: How to Tell Which One You Are Actually Dealing With

A factory quotes your product cost plus a margin it controls. A trading company buys that same product, adds its own margin, and adds handling and risk on top. The unit price looks similar, so how do you tell which one you are actually dealing with before your money moves?

Why This Matters When You Order Drinkware

Drinkware looks like a simple product, but the decisions made before production are what separate a supplier who delivers what you ordered from one who delivers something close. Capacity, steel grade, wall thickness, lid type and packaging all affect what lands in your warehouse, and most of the expensive mistakes are made before a single unit is produced. Buyers who treat sourcing as a checklist rather than a negotiation end up paying twice: once for goods that do not sell, and again for the reorder.

What a Reliable Process Looks Like

A factory that works with export buyers should be able to answer specific questions without hesitating: what material grade is used inside and outside the wall, what is the unit weight, what tolerance do they hold on capacity, what does the packaging consist of, and what happens if the goods fail inspection. If those answers are vague, the risk is not with you yet, but it will be later.

Written specification is what makes the order enforceable. A photo of a sample is not a specification. Attach a drawing or a written specification sheet, keep a sealed approved sample on both sides, and reference it in the purchase order. When something goes wrong, that document is the difference between a conversation and a claim.

The Questions to Ask Before You Commit

Ask for the material certificate covering your production batch rather than a generic document from another product. Ask who owns the tooling if you fund it. Ask what the defect tolerance is and who inspects before the container is sealed. Ask for the total delivered cost including packaging, inspection, inland trucking and duty, because a unit price on its own is not comparable between suppliers.

Ask for these answers in writing before paying a deposit. A supplier who answers precisely and commits to it in a contract is a different kind of partner from one who agrees to everything immediately and asks for money first.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most frequent sourcing errors are avoidable. Quoting from a low-quality photograph instead of a specification leads to substitutions. Comparing quotations that omit different line items leads to false comparisons. Approving a sample without a written record leads to arguments during production. And leaving quality terms out of the contract leads to disputes after the container has sailed, when your leverage is weakest.

None of these are complicated. They simply require a buyer who asks the second question.

Quick FAQ

These are the questions buyers ask most often on this topic.

  • Can I ask a supplier whether they are a factory or a trading company? You should ask directly, but verify the answer rather than trusting it. A genuine factory will offer a live video walkthrough of the production floor, name the machines it owns, and allow a third-party auditor to inspect. A trading company will usually restrict you to a showroom, be vague about machinery, and discourage third-party inspection.
  • Is a trading company ever a better choice than a factory? Yes, occasionally. If you order small quantities across many categories, a buying office or sourcing agent can consolidate multiple suppliers into one container and handle inspection and freight for you. The trade-off is a commission, typically 3-5%, and less control over which actual factory makes your goods.
  • Why do factories quote lower than trading companies? A factory quotes its own production cost plus a margin it controls. A trading company buys from a factory, adds its own margin, and adds handling and risk. For a simple logo-printed tumbler that markup can be 15-30%, or several times more on orders that involve multiple suppliers.
