Why Your Quoted Price Is Not What You Pay: Hidden Costs in China Sourcing

The unit price is the easiest number to compare and the least useful one. Tooling, samples, packaging, printing, inspection, inland trucking, ocean freight, duty and demurrage all sit outside it, and together they can add 30 to 45 percent to what you thought you negotiated. On a typical private-label order the FOB price is barely two-thirds of the money you actually spend before the goods sit in your warehouse.

This is not an argument that suppliers overcharge. It is an argument that the FOB price is not the price. Buyers who compare quotations on unit price alone are frequently not comparing two quotations at all: they are comparing a quote with a white box included against a quote with a colour box, a new mould and printing on both sides. The gap between the cheapest-looking quote and the real landed cost is usually larger than the gap between the cheapest and the second-cheapest quote.

Below is the complete cost stack for drinkware, line by line, with a worked example on real carton data, the traps that make a low quotation expensive, and the exact wording to use when you ask a supplier to break the price down.

What the Unit Price Actually Covers

A FOB quotation for drinkware normally covers the product itself, in the packaging the supplier stocks, undecorated or with the printing that was in the sample you approved. That is it. It does not normally cover a new mould, printed colour boxes, third-party inspection, trucking from the factory to the port, the export declaration, ocean freight, insurance, import duty, customs clearance at destination, or the storage charges that start the moment a container sits still too long.

For the sake of clarity, the way we describe ourselves matters here. STWADD is a factory-backed supply company, not a reseller: we are based in Yongkang, Zhejiang, and we produce to our customer's specification through audited partner factories we qualify and work with directly. When we quote, we tell you which cost lines sit inside the unit price and which sit outside it, and we quote the same way for every customer. That is not an unusual position to take, but it is unusual enough that buyers are often surprised by it, and it is the reason we are willing to publish the numbers below.

Two things are worth stating at the outset because they change the arithmetic rather than the description. First, a quotation has a shelf life: it depends on steel prices, labour, exchange rates and the factory's order book at the moment it was written, and it should be re-confirmed in the contract rather than treated as a standing offer. Second, most of the cost lines below are negotiated, not fixed. The question is never whether a cost exists. It is whether you knew about it before you paid a deposit.

One-Time Costs and Recurring Costs Are Different Animals

The most expensive line for a first-time buyer is tooling, and the most expensive mistake is to treat it as part of the unit price. Tooling is a one-time engineering cost, normally quoted separately and normally not refundable. It does not repeat. Once the mould exists, the second order pays only the production price, and the tooling cost amortises to zero across the rest of the programme.

That distinction changes the comparison entirely, and it is where a lot of buyers talk themselves into a bad decision. A supplier quoting a slightly higher unit price because they are absorbing a mould cost can look expensive on order one and be the cheapest option by order three. A supplier quoting a low unit price and USD 4,000 of tooling up front can look like a bargain and then quietly stop being one if the second order gets delayed, the design changes or the volume never justifies the tool.

The reverse trap is worse. A supplier who quotes a unit price that already includes an amortised tooling share, without saying so, will keep charging that share on every reorder. If you are comparing two quotations, you have to know for each one whether the number in front of you contains tooling or not, and if it does, how it was calculated. Our own schedule is published as a flat one-time fee rather than amortised into the unit price, so that a re-order cost drops by the full amount of the tool.

There is a second recurring-versus-one-time trap in printing. Spray coating setup and screen printing setup are per production run, not per year. A buyer who approves six colours for the launch will be charged six setup fees again on every reorder, whether or not the artwork has changed. Ask for the setup fee per colour per run, in writing, and factor it into the reorder cost rather than discovering it on the second invoice.

The Container Maths Buyers Skip

The carton is the least examined specification in the whole order, and it controls a surprising share of your freight bill. Ocean freight is charged per container, not per piece, so the number of pieces that fit in a container is what determines your freight cost per unit. Two identical products packed in different cartons can land in the same container at very different per-unit freight.

This is not theoretical. Take two real 1000ml vacuum bottle carton configurations from our own catalogue and put them in the same 40HQ. The internal dimensions of a 40ft high cube are 12.032 by 2.352 by 2.690 metres, which is 76.4 cubic metres of theoretical volume and about 65 to 68 cubic metres once you allow for gaps, stacking constraints and the space pallets and securing take up.

76 x 48 x 31 cm, 40 pcs per carton, 20 kg48019,20054.3 CBM (71 percent)USD 0.19
80 x 42 x 31 cm, 50 pcs per carton, 21.6 kg60030,00062.5 CBM (82 percent)USD 0.12

Same container, same freight invoice of USD 3,650, same destination. The second carton configuration delivers 56 percent more units and cuts freight per piece by more than a third. Nothing about the bottle changed. The packing did.

Two practical rules follow. First, ask for pieces per carton, carton dimensions in centimetres and gross carton weight, then do the division yourself before you accept a freight quote. A supplier who cannot give you carton dimensions is quoting you a container you cannot price. Second, watch the weight as well as the volume. A 40HQ carries about 26 tonnes maximum and 24 tonnes is the practical ceiling once you allow for the container's own tare; a light vacuum bottle hits the weight limit before it hits the volume limit, and a heavy one hits it much sooner. A 1000ml bottle at 20 kg per carton of 40 pieces is 500 grams per bottle, and 480 of those cartons is 9.6 tonnes, so this particular product is volume-limited and still leaves 30 percent of the container's cube unused. That unused space is money you have already paid for.

Import VAT is on a different footing from everything else in that table. It is a cash cost on the day the container clears, and it is recoverable if your business is registered for VAT in the destination country. It affects your working capital, not your margin, and it should be modelled separately rather than buried in a landed cost that nobody can audit.

The duty line is worth pausing on, because it is the one place where a cheap quotation can become an expensive one through a mistake rather than an omission. Vacuum flasks sit under heading 9617, and in the EU the third-country duty rate on 9617 00 00 is 6.7 percent; in the United Kingdom the same line carries 0 percent duty with 20 percent import VAT on top. Into the United States, a CBP classification ruling confirmed HTSUS 9617.00.1000 at 7.2 percent general rate for vacuum vessels of one litre or less, and confirmed separately that products of China under Chapter 99 heading 9903.01.24 attract an additional 20 percent ad valorem, so the real number on a US entry is not the 7.2 percent in the tariff schedule. Because Chapter 99 overlays change and exclusions get opened and closed, confirm the current provision and whether your product qualifies for an exclusion with your broker before you commit a landed-cost number. Why the Cheapest Quote Is Often the Most Expensive Order

There is a second category of gap that no cost breakdown will reveal, because it is buried inside the product specification rather than added on top of it. The quotation is honest about what it contains. The question is whether what it contains is what you ordered. Six substitutions account for almost all of it.

SubstitutionHow it shows upHow to catch it
201 stainless in place of 304Cheaper steel, lower nickel content, weaker corrosion resistance, wrong behaviour with acidic drinksPut the grade in the contract and ask for a material report on the production batch. A magnet rules 201 in or out but cannot confirm 304, and cannot detect a thin 304 shell over a 201 body

None of these is exotic. They are ordinary commercial shortcuts, and they are much more likely at the bottom of the price list than the top, because that is where the margin has to come from. A quote 35 percent below every other quotation deserves questions rather than excitement. Ask for the steel grade certificate, the wall thickness, the lid specification, the unit weight, the carton dimensions and the piece count per carton, and compare the answers against the other quotes line by line. The Costs You Only Discover After the Container Leaves

Some lines do not appear on any quotation because they only trigger when something goes wrong. They are worth pricing in advance anyway, because a buyer who knows the rate can make a decision, while a buyer who is surprised can only react.

Demurrage is the one that catches people. It is the charge for a loaded container sitting inside the terminal beyond the carrier's free time, and detention is the charge for the empty sitting outside it. Free time at major US and European ports is typically three to seven days from the vessel discharge date, and published 2026 rates cluster around USD 100 to 300 per container per day in North America and USD 100 to 250 in Europe, with escalation built in: the first days over free time commonly run USD 75 to 150, days six to ten USD 150 to 250, and beyond that USD 250 to 500 or more. Five extra days on a single 40HQ is therefore a plausible USD 750 to 1,500, and the same delay across a ten-container consignment is an invoice nobody budgeted for. The causes are almost always documentation: a mismatched commercial invoice, an HS code that does not match the description, a missing certificate. That is why the invoice description and the HS code have to agree before the container sails, and why pre-filing the import entry is worth the broker's fee.

Two smaller lines deserve a mention. The first is currency: a quotation denominated in dollars is exposed between the day you accept it and the day you pay, and if it is denominated in yuan it is exposed between the day you pay and the day the goods land, because your landed cost rises when the dollar falls against the currency you are selling into. The second is the interest cost of the cycle. A 30 percent deposit locks cash for four to eight months before the goods sell, and a first-time importer with one or two suppliers carries that working capital across every order simultaneously. Buyers who model the real cost of the programme include the deposit's cash cost alongside the unit price, and negotiate a smaller deposit where they can.

Finally, a delay on your side is not free either. Missing the vessel cut-off means the container misses the sailing and the goods move to the next one, which in peak season can cost three to four weeks of shelf life. Our guide to incoterms and who bears what covers who is responsible for booking the vessel and the cut-off date, which is a clause worth having in the purchase order rather than an assumption.

How to Ask for a Quote You Can Actually Compare

Most buyers ask for "your best price for 20,000 pieces" and get a number. The number is not wrong, but it is incomplete in a way that makes comparison impossible. Here is the wording that gets a full breakdown back. Send it as written, and expect the supplier to either fill it in or decline to.

Please quote the following line by line, for the specification attached, at [quantity] pcs:

1. Unit price, and state the Incoterm and named place (FOB Ningbo, Incoterms 2020).
2. Steel grade for body and liner, wall thickness, capacity tolerance, unit weight.
3. Lid type, gasket material, and whether the listed components are included.
4. Printing method, number of colours, setup fee per colour per run, and labour per piece.
5. Packaging: white box or colour box, and what is included (carton, insert, master carton, shipping marks).
6. Tooling: itemised one-time cost per tool, and confirm whether it is amortised into the unit price or quoted separately.
7. Samples: cost per piece, whether refunded or credited against the first order, and courier cost.
8. MOQ per design and per colour, and whether mixed designs in one container are allowed.
9. Pieces per carton, carton dimensions in cm, and gross carton weight in kg.
10. Production lead time, and the lead time for a reorder with the same specification.
11. Third-party inspection: cost per man-day, and whether it is included or additional.
12. Inland trucking to the named port, and the export declaration and documentation charge.
13. Validity period of the quotation, and the price basis if the steel price moves more than a stated amount.

Then add the one that does most of the work: please state separately which of the above are included in the unit price and which are additional, so that we can compare your quotation against the other quotations we are working with. That sentence does two things. It gives the supplier an easy, non-accusatory reason to itemise, and it makes clear that a price that cannot be decomposed will not be accepted. Suppliers who itemise properly are telling you they have a real cost structure. Suppliers who refuse are telling you something else.

Writing Cost Transparency Into the Purchase Order

A breakdown in an email is a request. A clause in the purchase order is an obligation. This is the part worth copying, because every omission here becomes an argument after the container has sailed, when your leverage is weakest.

Quotation and price basis clause: The unit price of USD [ ] per piece is FOB Ningbo, Incoterms 2020, calculated on the specification in Annex A, and excludes the items listed in Annex C (tooling, printing setup, colour boxes, third-party inspection, inland trucking, export documentation and export packing). Annex C lists each of those as a separate line with its quantity and rate. Tooling items are one-time charges for the buyer's property and are not charged again on reorders of the same item. The unit price is based on the steel price of [ ] on [date] and will be re-confirmed in this contract. A price validity of [ ] days applies from the date of this order.

Cost and quantity clause: The accepted quantity is [ ] pieces plus or minus 3 percent at the seller's option, any shortfall being credited at the unit price. The seller shall pack [ ] pieces per carton, carton size [ ] x [ ] x [ ] cm, gross weight [ ] kg per carton, with shipping marks and packing list as specified in Annex B. Final carton and piece count shall be verified at the loading gate in the presence of the buyer's appointed inspector, and the packing list shall match the physical count.

Specification clause: The goods shall conform to the golden sample signed by both parties and to Annex A, including 304 stainless steel grade for the body and liner, the specified wall thickness, the specified lid and gasket, and the printing method and colour count stated in Annex A. Any change to the steel grade, the liner material, the lid component or the wall thickness requires the buyer's prior written approval. Rejection rights for non-conforming goods apply notwithstanding any inspection or acceptance of the goods.

Three of those lines carry most of the weight. The excluded-items annex is what prevents the "the white box was included" argument a year later. The carton clause is what protects your freight rate, because the freight is calculated from the volume that the carton defines. And the specification clause with a prior-approval requirement is what makes a 201-for-304 substitution a breach rather than a negotiation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What costs are buyers usually surprised by? The most common surprises are tooling or mould fees assumed to be free, sample charges, custom packaging and printing, third-party inspection fees, inland trucking from factory to port, and demurrage if the container sits too long. Together these can add 15-40% to the unit price you negotiated.

Is a much lower quote always suspicious? Not always; volume and design simplicity explain a lot. But an outlier 30-40% below every other quote deserves questions, not excitement. Ask for the steel grade certificate, wall thickness, lid specification and unit weight. The most common trick is quoting 201 stainless instead of 304, or a thinner wall than the sample.

Can packaging and printing be included in the unit price? It depends on the factory. Some include a standard white box at no cost, others charge per unit. Always ask for the price broken into product, printing, packaging and tooling so you are comparing like with like across suppliers.

How much does a drinkware product actually cost once it is in my warehouse? For a typical private-label order of 19,200 vacuum bottles, the FOB unit price is usually 30 to 45 percent of the final landed cost per unit once tooling, printing, colour boxes, inspection, inland trucking, ocean freight, duty and clearance are added. Import VAT sits on top of that but is recoverable if you are registered, so it affects cash flow rather than cost. This is why the FOB number alone is a poor basis for deciding whether a supplier is cheap.

Should I ask suppliers for an all-in landed quote instead of a unit price? Yes, but only as a cross-check. Ask for the unit price broken into product, printing, packaging, tooling, samples, inspection, inland trucking and export documentation, then add ocean freight, duty and clearance yourself using your own broker rates. A supplier who refuses to break the price down is telling you something important, and an all-in quote you cannot audit is no better than a bare unit price.
