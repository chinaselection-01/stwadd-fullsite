When a China Buying Office Beats Factory Direct — and When It Does Not

Sometimes a China buying office is the cheapest way to buy, and sometimes it is an expensive way to do something a factory would have done for free. The difference is not the quality of the agent. It is arithmetic: how many factories your goods come from, how much of them fits in one container, and how many hours of your own time the coordination eats. Below is the honest comparison, with the numbers laid out so you can run them against your own order.

The Decision Is Not "Agent or No Agent" — It Is How Many Suppliers You Have

Buyers tend to frame this as a binary: do I hire a sourcing agent or do I go straight to the factory? That framing hides the only variable that matters. If your order comes from one factory, a buying office can only add a percentage and a layer of communication. If your order comes from six factories, a buying office can change the cost structure of the shipment entirely, because the fixed costs of moving goods out of China get paid once instead of six times.

That is the whole argument. Everything else — the supplier database, the AI matching, the multi-language RFQ drafting, the factory audits — matters, but it is secondary to the simple fact that a buyer with no one on the ground in China will spend either money or time on coordination, and the buying office sells you the version where you spend money.

So the first question is not which service to hire. It is how many factories are producing your order, and whether the volume you can commit to each one gets you a container of your own.

What a Buying Office Actually Does — and What It Does Not Do

A working buying office does eight things, and it is worth being precise about them because two of them are frequently claimed and rarely delivered:

1. Supplier identification and screening. Finding factories that actually make your product, checking whether their claims about capacity and certification survive a phone call, and narrowing a list of hundreds down to a handful worth quoting. 2. Specification writing. Turning a target product into a document a Chinese factory can quote against without guessing. 3. RFQ management. Issuing the request for quotation, chasing the replies, and normalising the responses so prices are comparable line by line. 4. Sample coordination. Getting samples made, shipped, and evaluated against a written specification. 5. Price negotiation. Arguing the number down and settling the terms. 6. Production follow-up. Confirming the goods are actually being made, on schedule, to the approved sample. 7. Inspection. Arranging third-party inspection at AQL 2.5 and acting on the report. 8. Consolidation and freight. Moving goods from several factories into one container, booking ocean freight, and preparing export documentation.

What a buying office does not do: make the product, own the tooling, take responsibility for your customs entry at destination, or guarantee that the goods will sell. And here is the distinction that matters more than any of the eight: an agent never takes title to the goods. A trading company buys the goods from the factory and resells them to you, earning on the spread. An agent represents you, negotiates on your behalf, and never owns the goods. If the counterparty on your invoice has taken title, you are dealing with a trading company and the fee structure is completely different — see our guide to telling a factory apart from a trading company for the tests that separate them.

The Three Paths, Honestly Named

There are three realistic ways to buy a mixed range from China, and each one has a different cost shape. This is the framework for the rest of the article, so it is worth reading carefully before the numbers.

Path 1 — Direct, single factory. You find one factory, place one purchase order, pay one deposit, and receive one container. There is no commission and no agent fee. You are exposed to that one factory's capacity, its quality, and its willingness to fix a problem after the container sails.

Path 2 — Direct, multiple factories, consolidated by your freight forwarder. You find several factories yourself, you pay each one, and your forwarder collects the cartons at each factory and consolidates them into one container at their warehouse. No commission, no buying-office fee — but you still have to qualify the factories, write the specifications, chase the quotes, follow production and handle the claims. That is your time, and it is the cost the buying office is actually charging you to absorb.

Path 3 — Buying office. The buying office takes over the coordination work in Path 2 and charges a percentage of order value, commonly 3% to 5%. In return, you get supplier qualification, quote comparison, production follow-up, inspection, and consolidation, plus one accountable party when something goes wrong.

The interesting part is that Path 3 is often barely more expensive than Path 2, because the consolidation savings are large. But Path 3 is often much more expensive than Path 1, and Path 1 is the right answer more often than buyers expect.

Where the Money Goes: Per-Shipment Costs vs Per-Supplier Costs

This is the distinction that makes the whole comparison tractable. Every cost in a China import falls into one of two buckets, and only one of them multiplies when you add suppliers.

Per-shipment costs are paid once per consignment and do not care how many factories are inside it: ocean freight, terminal handling and sealing, the export declaration, the ISF filing in the US, customs entry and brokerage at destination, and destination delivery. If the goods move as one consignment, you pay this stack once.

Per-supplier costs are paid once per factory and multiply immediately: the factory-to-port trucking move, the factory-to-warehouse delivery, the export declaration, the inspection visit, and every hour of RFQ chasing, sample coordination and production follow-up.

Two consequences follow, and they are the whole commercial argument.

First, per-supplier costs are the ones a buying office can actually remove. A good buying office delivers factories to a single consolidation warehouse, batches inspections across several factories in one trip, and issues one document set for the consignment. That collapses per-shipment costs toward one and removes most per-supplier logistics.

Second, per-CBM pricing punishes you for splitting shipments. LCL ocean freight on the China to US West Coast lane runs about $150 to $250 per CBM all-in, while an FCL 40HQ is a flat $1,800 to $3,500 regardless of how full it is. A 40HQ holds 76 CBM internally and 55 to 60 CBM of real cartons once you account for practical loading. Splitting the same 38 CBM into six shipments of 6.3 CBM each means paying the per-CBM rate six times over.

That is not a small difference. It is the difference between a $7,600 ocean bill and a $2,400 one for the same goods.

A retail buyer is assembling a private-label range across six factories: three drinkware lines plus three household lines, none of which individually fills a container. Consolidating saved $7,816 on this order. That saving is not a favour and not a negotiation — it is what the per-CBM versus flat-rate structure of ocean freight does when you stop splitting one container into six.

Now add the buying office. At 3% of $46,500 that is $1,395, giving a total of $7,713 — still $6,421 below Path A. At 5% it is $2,325, giving $8,643, still $5,491 below Path A. The commission is comfortably affordable here, and the reason is that consolidation did the heavy lifting.

But look at what the commission is actually buying, because this is the honest part. If your freight forwarder already collects at each factory and consolidates into one container, it has already captured most of the $7,816. What the additional 3% to 5% buys is supplier qualification, comparable quoting, production follow-up, inspection management, and one accountable party for claims. Whether that is worth $2,325 is a question about your time, and we will give you the arithmetic in a moment.

The non-monetary savings in Path B are real too. LCL adds 3 to 7 days at the origin CFS and 2 to 4 days at destination deconsolidation, so a 22 to 32 day door-to-door journey becomes 18 to 24 days for a full container. One container means one ETA, one arrival, one inspection window at your warehouse instead of six staggered waves. Damage rates on LCL run two to four times higher than FCL, because the goods are handled repeatedly, and your drinkware packaging does not survive that for free. And because six separate LCL consignments sit in six separate containers, a customs examination on someone else's cargo cannot hold yours — unless your cargo happens to be sharing one of those containers.

The Single-SKU Case, Where Direct Always Wins

Now the other end of the range. One factory, one product, a first order of 5,000 pieces. Take a real model from our range: the 500ml triple-layer 304 stainless steel tumbler, packed 50 pieces to a carton of 72 × 37 × 25 cm, 15.8 kg per carton. That is 0.00133 CBM per piece, so 5,000 pieces is 6.65 CBM in 100 cartons and 1.58 tonnes — 8.7% of a 40HQ.

At 8.7% of a container you cannot take an FCL rate, so this ships LCL. The logistics bill comes to roughly $2,419: $1,330 ocean at $200 per CBM, $115 origin flat charges, $250 trucking, $70 declaration, $419 inspection, $50 ISF and $185 brokerage. That is $0.48 per piece before duties.

Now put the buying office against that. At an illustrative FOB of $2.60 per piece the order is $13,000, so a 5% commission is $650 — which is 27% of the entire logistics and inspection bill. If your unit price is $3.40 rather than $2.60, the same arithmetic makes the commission $850, or 35% of that bill. You would be paying a fifth of your product price for coordination work that a single factory, a single purchase order and one email thread handle perfectly well.

And the arithmetic gets worse, not better, on the reorder. The second order from the same factory is where your leverage should be strongest: you know exactly what you want, you have a proven sample, and a good factory will sharpen the price because repeat volume is worth more to them than the first order. Inserting a buying office between you and that factory means you are negotiating your second order through someone else's relationship, and the commission does not shrink — it scales with the order value.

The same logic applies to custom tooling. If you are funding a new mould, the value of the relationship is not the price of one container — it is who owns the mould and who answers the phone when it needs repair. The Break-Even Arithmetic You Can Run in Five Minutes

Here is the formula, stripped down. Take your order value V, the number of suppliers S, the commission rate c, and the per-supplier cost you would otherwise absorb F — inspection visits, separate trucking moves, separate declarations, and your own coordination hours.

Commission cost = V × c
Cost you avoid = (S − 1) × F + hours you would have spent × your hourly cost

Using the worked example above: at 5% on $46,500, the commission is $2,325. At $50 per hour of your own time, that commission buys you 46 hours of back — which is roughly what qualifying six factories, normalising six quotations, running samples, chasing production updates and handling one inspection cycle actually takes. At $75 an hour it buys 31 hours. If the whole coordination would have taken you 15 hours, you should order direct and hire a freight forwarder to consolidate.

Now run it for a single factory: S = 1, so the avoided per-supplier cost is zero. The formula collapses to "does the commission buy you back more hours than the coordination would take?" For one factory, one product, one purchase order, it does not. That is the entire case for going direct, and it applies to a lot of orders that people pay agents for.

Two variables will shift the result and both are worth checking. First, the spread between single-factory and multi-supplier orders — one supplier means direct, four or more usually means the consolidation maths dominates. Second, your repeat rate: a buying office cost on the first order is a learning cost you pay once. If you reorder the same range every quarter, the amortised cost per order falls, and the answer changes. If it was a one-off promotion, it does not.

When Factory Direct Is Cheaper, and Should Be

Be blunt about this list, because it is longer than most buying office websites will tell you.

One factory, one product. A commission has nothing to amortise across.

Repeat orders from a supplier you already trust. The relationship is the asset, and an intermediary weakens it.

You are funding new tooling. You want a direct line to the tooling engineer and a direct line on the ownership clause.

Your freight forwarder already consolidates. Plenty of forwarders with their own China warehouse will collect from multiple factories and load one container for a couple of hundred dollars. In that case the buying office is charging you mainly for supplier qualification and production follow-up, which is a real service — but price it as that service, not as a logistics necessity.

You already have someone in China. If a colleague, a friend or a part-time employee can visit the factory and watch production, the commission buys you something you can get for a plane ticket.

The order value is small. Below roughly $10,000 of goods, 5% is $500 and the fixed logistics stack dominates. There is no scenario where the percentage is the problem, and at that order size it is not the main cost either — but there is also very little left to coordinate, so there is little to pay for.

You need a hard answer on a defect claim. When a container arrives with 8% of the lids failing the leak test, an agent between you and the factory will negotiate on your behalf. That is exactly when you would rather be on the phone with the factory yourself, with the inspection report and the approved sample in front of both of you.

When a Buying Office Earns Its Fee

Equally, there are cases where Path 3 is clearly right, and it is worth being clear about them too.

Multiple categories, multiple factories, each below container volume. This is the canonical buying-office case, and the worked example above is exactly it. Consolidation converts per-CBM freight into a flat rate, and no buyer without a consolidation warehouse can do that for themselves.

Your first order from a country you do not know. If you have never imported from China, you do not have a supplier you trust, a forwarder who answers, or a feel for what a reasonable quote looks like. The first order is expensive in mistakes, and mistakes are not priced as a percentage.

Compliance-heavy ranges. Food-contact, toy safety and electrical categories have documentation requirements that are not negotiable and are not your strength. Our guide to FDA, LFGB and Prop 65 for drinkware covers the specific chain for drinkware; for other categories, someone who already knows the documentation sequence saves you a container's worth of detention risk.

You cannot commit to a volume that gets you a container. Factory minimums can be brutal. If the minimum from a good factory is 3,000 pieces and you only need 800, that factory will not take the order, and your alternative is a trading company. A buying office can place the small order with a factory that accepts it, or pool your 800 pieces with other buyers' orders to reach a workable quantity.

Volume you cannot forecast. Seasonal ranges, promotional runs and marketplace-driven replenishment reward a partner who can hold and stage stock against your actual sell-through, rather than a factory that only sells in container quantities.

The Question That Reveals Everything: Who Pays the Agent?

Before anything else, establish how the buying office is compensated, because there are three possibilities and one of them is corrosive.

You pay the commission. Clean, disclosed and simple. The agent's incentive is aligned with yours on order value — which means they are rewarded when you buy more, and paid nothing for driving your unit price down.

The factory pays a commission or rebate. This is the arrangement that destroys trust, and it is common enough that you should assume it exists until the invoice is shown to you. A factory rebate of 3% to 5% hidden inside the quoted price means you are paying twice: the agent's fee on top, and an unknown markup inside the unit price. If the agent will not show you the factory's actual invoice so you can see the markup, you cannot verify whether you are paying a fair price, and you certainly cannot renegotiate it.

They take both. Ask directly, in writing: "Does our factory know we are paying you, and does the price you quoted include any commission paid to you by the factory?" The answer tells you which kind of relationship you are in.

Industry commentary is blunt about this: a low commission paid alongside an undisclosed factory rebate costs more than a higher fee paid openly. The way to protect yourself is not to hunt for the cheapest agent. It is to require open-book pricing — the factory's invoice, visible, with your name and your specifications on it.

Seven Questions to Ask Before You Sign With a Buying Office

Ask these in writing, before any deposit, and compare the answers rather than the brochures.

1. Will you show us the factory's invoice? If the answer is no, you are paying an unknown price for an unknown factory and there is no reason to continue.

2. Which activities are inside the fee, and which are billed separately? Sample cost, tooling, inspection, freight, documentation. Get the exclusions in writing, because the exclusions are where the cost appears.

3. Do you take title to the goods? If yes, you are dealing with a trading company, and you should read our comparison of the two structures before signing.

4. Who holds the tooling, and in whose name? If you are funding a mould, you want it in your name or at a factory you can compel to release it. Get the clause, do not rely on the relationship.

5. What happens if goods fail inspection? Who files the claim, in what currency, within what deadline, and is the factory contractually obliged to refund or replace? A clause naming the deadline is worth more than a sympathetic promise.

6. May we contact the factory directly? An agent who forbids this is protecting a relationship, not your interests.

7. What is the deliverable timeline, in writing, and who owns the delay? Sample date, production completion date, ready date, vessel, ETA. If the agent will not put dates on paper, the schedule is not a commitment.

What to Put in the Contract With a Buying Office

Whichever path you take, the contract needs to cover five things, and three of them are the ones buyers leave out.

The commission basis. State the rate, the base (order value before or after freight), the invoice currency, and the payment trigger. A commission on goods value is a different number from a commission on landed value, and the difference is not academic on a large order.

The factory invoice disclosure. A clause requiring the agent to provide the factory's commercial invoice, with the factory's name on it, within a defined number of days of shipment. This one clause does more for your pricing than any negotiation tactic, because it converts every future reorder into a negotiation against a number you can see.

Rebate disclosure. A clause stating that any commission or rebate received from the factory is disclosed and credited against the buyer's fee, or that none is received.

The tooling clause. Ownership of any mould or tooling the buyer funds, where it is stored, the maintenance obligation, and the release obligation if the buyer moves to another factory. Our mould cost guide has the numbers behind each option if you are budgeting the tooling side.

The claims clause. Inspection standard, the reporting deadline, who files the claim, the remedy (rework, replacement, refund), and the jurisdiction. This has to be in the agreement with the factory as well as with the agent, because the agent has no money of its own to refund you with.

The Middle Path: Buy Your Core Direct, Consolidate the Rest

Most buyers treat sourcing as one decision and pick one model for everything. That is where the money leaks, because your range is rarely uniform. In practice it is usually two or three high-volume core lines plus a long tail of smaller categories, and the two halves have completely different optimal structures.

The core lines — the ones that fill a container, repeat every quarter, and need your custom mould — should be bought direct. The commission buys you nothing there and the direct relationship is what protects you on price and on defects.

The long tail — kitchen storage, household tools, seasonal items, the SKUs that make a retail range look complete — should be consolidated. These are the lines where per-supplier costs multiply and consolidation pays for itself, and where nobody at your business has the time or the Chinese-language contacts to run six small orders properly.

This is also the honest answer to a question buyers rarely ask: the cost of a buying office is not spread evenly across your order. It is concentrated in the small, many-supplier lines, and it is negative in the big, single-supplier ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is a sourcing agent cheaper than ordering from a factory direct? A buying office pays off when you buy across several categories, when quantities per factory are small, when you have no one in China to inspect and consolidate, or when you cannot commit to a large single-factory order. If you are ordering one product in volume from one plant, direct is almost always cheaper. In the worked example above, consolidating six suppliers into one container saved $7,816 — more than the $2,325 commission at 5%. With one supplier there is nothing to consolidate, so the commission buys only coordination that a purchase order and a phone call already handle.

What does a buying office actually do that I cannot do myself? Matching your specification to qualified suppliers, automating RFQs, comparing quotes side by side, screening compliance and landed cost, consolidating multiple suppliers into one container, running AQL 2.5 inspection, arranging private-label packaging and booking freight to your port. The value is coordination, not a secret source of cheaper products. The measurable part is the logistics consolidation; the judgement part is supplier qualification and production follow-up, and you should price the two separately when you compare quotes.

How transparent should the commission be? A buying office should state the commission up front, typically 3-5% of order value, and show you the factory's actual invoice so you can see the markup. If they refuse to disclose the factory or the underlying invoice, you cannot verify whether you are paying a fair price. Ask one question in writing: does the price include any commission paid to us by the factory? A clear no, plus an invoice you can read, is the standard you should hold anyone to.

Will my freight forwarder do the same job more cheaply? Often, yes — and you should ask. Plenty of forwarders with their own warehouse in China will collect from several factories, consolidate into one container and handle the export documents. That removes most of the per-shipment cost. What they typically will not do is qualify the factory, write the specification, normalise the quotes, follow production, or argue your claim when goods fail inspection. If your need is logistics only, use a forwarder and save the percentage; if your need is people on the ground, pay for that.

If I pay for moulds through a buying office, do I own them? You should, and you need it in writing rather than as a habit. The clause needs to name who owns the mould, where it is physically stored, who maintains it, and the exact conditions under which the factory must release it if you move elsewhere — including the condition it must be in and the deadline for release.
