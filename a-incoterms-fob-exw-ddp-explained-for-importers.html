EXW vs FOB vs DDP: Incoterms for Drinkware Imports and Who Bears What

EXW, FOB and DDP are not interchangeable. They decide who pays for inland trucking, ocean freight, insurance, duty and customs clearance, and who carries the risk at each step. Choosing the wrong one can quietly add thousands to a single container. It can also determine whether your container clears at the destination port at all, or sits on the quay while nobody can legally sign for it.

What an Incoterm Actually Controls, and What It Does Not

An Incoterm is a rule about delivery, not a contract. It fixes one thing: where the seller's obligation ends, where risk transfers to the buyer, and which costs each side pays along the way. It does not specify the product, the price, the payment terms, the quality standard or the inspection procedure. Those live in the purchase order, and the two documents have to agree with each other.

The current edition is Incoterms 2020, in force since 1 January 2020. It replaced Incoterms 2010 and changed several rules that buyers still work from old templates. Three matter for drinkware. First, FCA was amended so that you can instruct your carrier to issue an on-board bill of lading to the seller, which removed the main practical objection to using FCA on containers. Second, CIF still carries only minimum insurance cover, while CIP was raised to all-risks cover. Third, DAT was renamed DPU and can now apply at any place, not only at a terminal.

Always write the version into the contract. A contract that says only "FOB" is governed by whichever edition was in force when it was signed, which means an old term can be applied to a new shipment. Write "FOB Ningbo, Incoterms 2020" and the ambiguity disappears.

EXW: The Cheapest Quote and the Most Expensive Reality

EXW places the maximum obligation on the buyer and the minimum on the seller. The seller makes the goods available at its premises and that is the end of its responsibility. The buyer arranges everything else, and under the rules the buyer is also responsible for export clearance in the country of origin.

That last point is where EXW breaks down for most buyers sourcing from China. A foreign company cannot simply walk into a Chinese customs office and export a container of drinkware in its own name. The exporter of record has to be a Chinese entity with the right registration, and that entity has to hold the customs declaration, pay the export taxes and, critically, claim the export VAT refund.

For insulated drinkware that refund is significant. Chinese HS 9617 covers vacuum flasks and other vacuum vessels, and the export VAT rebate rate on those lines is 13 percent, matching the 13 percent VAT output rate. In practice, a factory that exports through its own name recovers that refund and can quote a clean FOB price. A factory asked to sell EXW to an overseas buyer, where the buyer is notionally the exporter of record, cannot claim the refund, so the price it quotes has to be higher, not lower, to compensate.

The result is that EXW from a Chinese factory is frequently a fiction: the price looks cheap because the export machinery has been quietly moved somewhere else, and the risk of everything going wrong at the port of loading still sits with you. If a supplier offers you EXW, ask a specific question: who is the exporter of record on the Chinese customs declaration, and on whose name is the VAT refund claimed? If the answer is vague, the quote is not saving you anything.

FOB: The Default, and the Gap Everyone Misses

FOB, Free On Board, is what most drinkware contracts use. The seller clears the goods for export, delivers them to the port of shipment and loads them on board the vessel. Risk passes to you once the goods are on board. From that point you pay the ocean freight, insurance, destination charges, import duty and delivery, and you handle clearance in your country.

For a breakbulk cargo or anything genuinely loaded onto a vessel under the seller's supervision, FOB works well. For a full container, it is the wrong rule, and the International Chamber of Commerce says so explicitly: FOB should not normally be used for container shipments, and sellers should use FCA instead.

The reason is a timing gap that only bites when something goes wrong. In a container shipment, the box is sealed, trucked to the port and handed over to the terminal. That happens days before the vessel sails. Under FOB the seller still carries the risk for that entire period, but the seller no longer controls the container and has no people on the ground watching it. If a container is damaged in the terminal yard, mis-stuffed, or sits through a typhoon, the paper still says the seller is responsible and neither party is in a position to settle it quickly.

Buyers often end up on FOB for a documentary reason rather than a commercial one: their letter of credit requires an on-board bill of lading, and under the old FCA rules a container handover produced a received-for-shipment bill instead, which the bank would refuse. Incoterms 2020 fixed exactly this. Under FCA you can now agree with your forwarder that the carrier issues an on-board bill of lading to the seller once the container is loaded. The commercial answer for containers is FCA, and the documentary obstacle that pushed everyone onto FOB no longer exists.

CIF, CIP and CFR: Insurance and Freight Without Moving the Risk

These three sit on the other side of the payment table. Under CFR and CIF the seller pays freight to the named port of destination, and under CIF the seller also buys insurance. What they do not do is move the risk. Under CFR and CIF, risk still passes to you when the goods are loaded on board at the port of shipment, exactly as under FOB. Buyers often assume that a seller paying the freight and the insurance means the seller owns the cargo until it arrives. It does not.

There is a second trap in CIF. The seller is required to insure at minimum cover, which corresponds to Institute Cargo Clauses C, a relatively narrow form that does not respond to many of the things that actually go wrong with drinkware in transit: handling damage, crushing, breakage of a glass-lined lid, theft from a container. CIP, which works for any transport mode, requires the wider all-risks cover under Institute Cargo Clauses A. If you want the seller to arrange insurance, ask which form is being written into the policy rather than accepting the word insurance.

DDP: Simple on Paper, and Where It Falls Apart

DDP, Delivered Duty Paid, is the maximum seller obligation. The seller delivers the goods to your named place, ready for unloading, having cleared them for import and paid the import duty and tax. The buyer receives one number and one door delivery. It is the easiest term for a buyer with no freight experience.

It is also the term with the most failure modes, because it requires something structurally difficult: the seller must be able to act as importer of record in your country. In the European Union that means registering for import VAT, since the seller becomes the party liable for the VAT on entry. In the United States it means either holding customs registration and posting a bond, or instructing a licensed broker to import on the seller's behalf. This is why a great many Chinese suppliers quote DDP through a freight forwarder rather than in their own name.

The practical consequence is that the entity clearing your container is not your counterparty and not you. If the customs authority queries the declared value, if the goods are held for a physical inspection, or if a marking or labelling requirement is missed, you have no standing in that conversation, and the forwarder's priority is its own exposure and its other customers. Many DDP quotes also bundle a customs clearance fee that is genuinely a broker charge dressed as part of the product price.

There is a second DDP trap specific to your paperwork. Under DDP the seller is the importer of record, which means the commercial invoice value, the customs declaration and the freight documents all have to be internally consistent. If a supplier quotes DDP on a low invoice value to reduce the duty, the declaration is wrong, the container is held, and the fine lands on whoever is the importer of record. Ask what the invoice value will be and compare it to the FOB price for the same goods. A DDP quote that is meaningfully below FOB plus freight plus duty is telling you something about how the numbers were built.

What Each Term Does to Your Landed Cost

Take a concrete order so the comparison has a scale. Suppose 20,000 stainless steel 20oz tumblers at a FOB Ningbo price of USD 3.10 each, which is USD 62,000 at the port. Ocean freight from Ningbo to Rotterdam runs roughly USD 3,500 on a 40ft container, and to US West Coast roughly USD 4,500 to 6,000, with insurance typically around 0.3 percent of the cargo value.

On to Europe, your customs position depends on the HS line. Vacuum flasks and other vacuum vessels sit under heading 9617. If the goods are declared as 9617 00 00 00, the EU third-country duty rate is 6.7 percent and import VAT applies at 23 percent in Germany, on a base of CIF value plus duty. That puts landed cost at roughly USD 86,000 including VAT, of which about USD 16,000 is import VAT that a VAT-registered buyer recovers later through their return. The same container into the United Kingdom attracts a third-country duty rate of 0 percent on 9617 00 00 00, with import VAT at 20 percent instead, so the cash outlay lands near USD 80,800.

On to the United States, the subheading matters more than the heading. A January 2026 CBP classification ruling placed a vacuum-insulated stainless steel bottle of one litre or less under HTSUS 9617.00.1000 at a general rate of 7.2 percent ad valorem, and confirmed separately that Chapter 99 trade remedy provisions may apply on top of that rate. Capacity, wall construction and whether the vessel is genuinely vacuum-insulated all change the subheading and therefore the rate, which is why a code copied from a catalogue rather than confirmed against your actual product is a liability.

Now compare the terms. Under FOB, your cash requirement is the USD 62,000 plus freight plus insurance, and the duty and VAT arrive as a separate bill at the border. Under DDP, the seller quotes one number that already contains freight, insurance, duty and often a clearance fee, and that number is typically higher than the sum of the parts once the seller's estimate of duty uses the highest plausible rate. The decisive question is not which number is smaller. It is whether you want to see the breakdown, because under DDP you cannot audit a number you cannot decompose, and you cannot tell whether the seller built it from the real duty rate or a worst-case one.

Why Drinkware Gets Flagged on HS Code

Insulated drinkware is unusually exposed to classification errors, and the errors are systematic rather than random. Heading 9617 covers vacuum flasks and other vacuum vessels, complete with cases, excluding glass inners. The presence of a genuine vacuum is what puts the product there, regardless of whether the outer shell is steel or plastic. The three codes buyers most often get wrong are 7323.93 for stainless steel table and kitchenware, 3924.10 for plastic tableware, and 7020 for a glass inner shipped as a separate piece. A plain non-vacuum steel mug is 7323.93. A plastic bottle that is not insulated is 3924.10. A glass liner for a vacuum flask, imported on its own, is 7020.

Two things follow. First, an error is not a rounding difference. The gap between the rate on the correct line and the rate on a neighbouring line can be several percentage points of the CIF value, which on a 62,000 dollar cargo is real money rather than a rounding argument. Second, under-declaring the code and value is treated as a false declaration, not as an administrative slip. Customs can hold the container for inspection, demand back duty with penalties, and in a serious case treat it as smuggling. Buyers have learned this the expensive way, which is why the code should be agreed with your broker before the container sails, not discovered afterwards.

The Export Declaration and the Tax Refund Chain

Under FOB the seller is responsible for export clearance, which in China means the seller files the customs declaration, pays the export taxes and claims the 13 percent VAT refund available on the relevant 9617 lines. The document trail from that declaration then follows the container: the declaration supports the export tax refund, and the commercial invoice and packing list must reconcile with it.

Here is the problem buyers in China-sourced supply chains run into repeatedly. The company named as exporter on the Chinese customs declaration is not always the company named as seller on your commercial invoice. Export houses frequently route the declaration through a related entity or a nominated agent while contracting with the offshore or holding entity that signs your purchase order. This is a common and legal structure, and it is also the reason the VAT refund chain works at all. But it creates a document set where the shipper on the bill of lading, the exporter on the Chinese declaration and the seller on your invoice are three different names, and any one of them can be checked by the customs authority in your country.

Two practical requests protect you. Ask which legal entity is the exporter of record on the Chinese declaration and confirm it appears consistently on the export declaration, the bill of lading and the commercial invoice, or ask for a written explanation of why the names differ and which document is which. And require the commercial invoice value to match the declared value on the customs paperwork, because a mismatch is the single most common trigger for a customs query on arrival.

How to Write the Term Into the Purchase Order

Write the term in a way that cannot be misread, and put it in the contract rather than in a covering email. State the rule, the version and the named place: FOB Ningbo, Incoterms 2020, or FCA Ningbo terminal, Incoterms 2020. Name the terminal rather than writing FCA China.

Then attach the terms that make the delivery obligation enforceable. A workable clause runs along these lines: goods delivered FCA Ningbo-Zhoushan terminal, Incoterms 2020, with the seller procuring export clearance and presenting an on-board bill of lading issued to the seller at the buyer's instruction; the seller bears all charges and risks up to handover of the goods to the carrier; the invoice value shall correspond to the customs declaration value; and the HS code shall be agreed in writing before production, with any change requiring the buyer's prior written approval.

Three lines carry most of the weight. The on-board bill of lading instruction is what makes FCA workable with a letter of credit. The invoice-to-declaration value match is what keeps the container out of a customs query. The HS code confirmation is what stops a late reclassification from landing on you as a back-duty bill with penalties. Add a separate clause naming who books the vessel and the cut-off date, because a missed cut-off on a container is a demurrage charge that arrives before anyone has opened the box.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between FOB and EXW? With EXW the seller hands the goods to you at the factory door, so you arrange and pay for everything including the export declaration. With FOB the seller delivers the goods loaded on the vessel at the port of shipment, and risk plus freight to that point sit with the seller. Most drinkware buyers use FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai.

Is DDP always cheaper for a buyer? No. DDP looks simple because everything is bundled into one price, but freight and duty are hidden in the unit price and you cannot see the breakdown. If your duty rate is high, DDP often costs more than FOB plus your own forwarder. Ask for FOB, duty and freight separately so you can compare.

What happens if the factory quotes FOB but the HS code is wrong? Customs may hold the container for inspection, demand back duty and issue a penalty if the declared value or code does not match the actual goods. This is common with drinkware because vacuum and non-vacuum bottles can fall under different tariff lines. Confirm the code with your broker before shipping.

Should I use FOB or FCA for a container shipment? For a full container, FCA is technically the better fit. Under FOB the seller carries risk until the container is on board the vessel, but in practice the seller loses control of the box when it reaches the export terminal, days before loading, so there is a gap where nobody is responsible. FCA transfers risk when the goods are handed to your carrier at the named terminal. Many buyers still use FOB because their letter of credit requires an on-board bill of lading; Incoterms 2020 resolves this by letting you instruct your carrier to issue an on-board bill of lading to the seller under FCA.

Who acts as importer of record under DDP, and what happens if the supplier cannot? Under DDP the seller is responsible for import clearance and duty, which in practice means the seller or its agent must be able to act as importer of record in your country. That requires a registered entity there or a broker willing to import on the seller's behalf. Where the supplier cannot arrange this, the container arrives with nobody legally able to clear it and is held. This is the most common reason a DDP quote turns into a customs problem.
